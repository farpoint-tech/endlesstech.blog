Why Backups Fail During a Ransomware Attack: Three Controls That Decide Recovery

November 2026 • By Philipp Schmidt
RansomwareBackupSecurityCase StudySMB

Backups fail during a ransomware attack for reasons that have little to do with whether the backup job ran. They fail when the backup target is reachable from the compromised network, when no copy is immutable or offline, and when nobody has tested a restore.

New Leadership, Old Blind Spots

Leadership changes and cost pressure often expose the same blind spot: the company depends on controls that were never documented as controls. A security subscription is cancelled, a single internal expert leaves, or an old task keeps running under an account nobody owns.

Nothing necessarily breaks on the day of the decision. That delay is the problem. An undocumented dependency can remain invisible until another incident makes it relevant.

Does Turning Off the Server Room AC for a Weekend Really Save Money?

Aging infrastructure creates the same kind of risk. A component that looks cheap to postpone can become expensive once it fails during a production period. The technical failure is only one part of the cost; the business interruption usually weighs more.

The Failure Chain

A phishing message, an unpatched endpoint, and a network that gives one compromised identity access to too much can create a short path from initial access to widespread encryption. There is no useful universal number of minutes; the point is that detection and response may not have the time people assume.

If the backup server shares the same trust boundary as production, it can become another victim. If the offline copy is missing or unverified, a green job report is not a recovery plan.

Ransom payment is not a recovery strategy. Decryption is not guaranteed, data may already have been taken, and the decision requires legal, insurance, and incident-response advice for the situation at hand.

So Why Do Backups Fail Exactly When You Need Them?

A backup can exist and still fail the moment it is needed. The two common false assumptions are that the backup is separate from what it protects and that a completed job proves a restore will work. Three properties decide whether a backup can support recovery. They are separate controls, and having one does not provide the others.

1. Network reachability of the backup target

Ransomware using compromised credentials can encrypt or delete what it can reach and authenticate to. Treat backup infrastructure as a separate security boundary: isolate administration, identities, and network paths from production as far as the design allows. That reduces exposure; it does not make backup copies tamper-proof on its own.

2. An immutable or air-gapped copy

Keep at least one copy that cannot be changed or deleted during its retention period, either because storage enforces immutability or because the media is physically disconnected. An offline copy only helps when the process for creating and verifying it is reliable.

3. A tested restore

A successful backup job tells you that selected data was copied without errors. It does not tell you whether the right data was selected, whether the copy can be read back, or how long recovery will take. Only a restore test in an isolated environment answers those questions.

Failures rarely arrive alone. A weak handover, an ageing component, a permissive network path, and an untested backup can remain separate problems until one incident joins them. Part 3 explains the cost categories that follow.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did the backup server get encrypted during the ransomware attack?

A backup server can be encrypted when it shares credentials, administration, or network reachability with compromised production systems. Treat backup infrastructure as a separate security boundary.

What is the difference between a successful backup job and a verified restore?

A successful backup job means the software copied the selected data and logged no errors. A verified restore means someone brought that data back into an isolated environment and confirmed it is complete and usable within an acceptable time.

What is a dead man's switch in IT, and why do departing admins leave them?

It is a script or scheduled task that keeps running, or stops something, after an admin leaves. More often than not, it is simply an undocumented task nobody thought to hand over. That is dangerous because nobody knows to look for it.

Should backup servers be domain-joined?

There is no one architecture for every environment. The goal is to keep backup administration, identities, and network paths separate from production wherever practical, and to retain at least one immutable or offline copy. Test restores to prove the design works.

How fast can ransomware encrypt a network?

There is no useful universal number. Encryption speed depends on the malware, permissions, network design, and the attacker's preparation. Do not build a recovery plan on the assumption that there will be hours to react.
