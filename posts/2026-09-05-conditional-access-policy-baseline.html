Conditional Access Policy Baseline: Cleaning Up 40 Policies Nobody Reviews

September 2026 • By Philipp Schmidt
Entra IDConditional AccessSecurityBest Practices

Open the Conditional Access blade in most tenants that have been running a few years and you will find somewhere between fifteen and fifty policies, named by whoever set them up that day, half of them in report-only mode with nobody checking the sign-in logs behind them.

This is not a training problem. It is structural: Microsoft ships Conditional Access as a policy engine, not a policy set. There is no shipped baseline saying "these are the policies a normal tenant needs." Every admin reinvents it, which is why the same question keeps coming back — someone inherits a tenant, opens the policy list, and cannot tell what half of it is for.

In short

Six policies cover the real baseline: MFA for all users, block legacy authentication, stricter MFA for admins, device compliance for sensitive apps, risk or location restriction, and session controls for unmanaged devices. Everything past that should map to a named risk. Report-only is a staging step with a deadline, not a permanent state — and a template copied across tenants without drift detection is a starting point, not a control.

What a minimum viable baseline actually covers

Stripped down, a working baseline covers a small, fixed set of conditions. Everything past that is tenant-specific hardening rather than foundation:

  1. Require MFA for all users — the non-negotiable starting point, scoped to all users with an exclusion group reserved for break-glass accounts only
  2. Block legacy authentication — protocols that cannot do modern auth cannot do MFA, so they are a guaranteed bypass path if left open
  3. Require MFA for admin roles specifically — a second, stricter layer on top of the general policy, usually with a shorter session lifetime
  4. Require a compliant or hybrid-joined device for sensitive apps — device-state gating for anything touching sensitive data, not just mail
  5. Restrict risky sign-ins or unfamiliar locations — the risk-based version needs Entra ID P2; a location allow-list is the P1-compatible fallback
  6. Session controls for unmanaged devices — restrict downloads or apply app-enforced restrictions for browser access from devices you do not manage

That is six policies, not forty. Anything beyond it should be a deliberate addition for a risk you can name — not something copied from a forum thread because it sounded reasonable.

Why report-only mode quietly stops being useful

Report-only is the right way to test a policy before enforcing it: it logs what would have happened without blocking anyone. The failure is not the feature, it is what happens next. A policy goes to report-only, the admin moves to the next fire, and eighteen months later it is still report-only because nobody built the habit of checking the logs behind it. At that point it is not a staged rollout — it is a policy that does nothing while looking, at a glance, like it does something.

If you have report-only policies older than a few weeks, the fix is not another policy. It is a scheduled fifteen-minute check against the Conditional Access insights and reporting workbook, followed by a deliberate decision to enforce or delete. A report-only policy with no decision date attached is not a control; it is a placeholder.

Testing before you flip the switch

The What If tool answers one question well: for a given user, app and set of conditions, which policies apply and what is the result. Run it against your admin accounts, your break-glass accounts and any service accounts before enforcing anything new — it catches the lockout scenario before it happens to a real person instead of after.

Consolidating without breaking things

Policy sprawl is rarely forty distinct intentions. It is usually five or six intentions duplicated across app-specific and group-specific variants that could be one policy with broader scope. Before deleting anything, export the current set (Graph identity/conditionalAccess/policies or the admin center export), group policies by the condition they actually test rather than by name, and look for duplicates differing only in target app or group. Those usually collapse into a single policy with a wider assignment.

MSPs: templates without drift detection are a false sense of consistency

Copying a policy template across customer tenants solves the initial deployment problem and creates a new one: each tenant drifts independently as admins make one-off changes, and nothing native flags "tenant B's MFA policy no longer matches the template it came from." Centralised tooling that reapplies the template and reports divergence closes that gap. A reference document does not — the document does not know when reality has moved away from it.

Bottom line

The absence of a Microsoft-shipped baseline is not a documentation gap you wait out. It is a standing decision every tenant has to make for itself. Six policies cover the foundation, report-only needs a deadline attached, and a copied template without drift detection is a starting point rather than an ongoing control.

Glossary

Conditional Access
The Entra ID policy engine that evaluates signals (user, device, location, risk) at sign-in and enforces allow, block, require MFA or require compliant device.
Report-only mode
A policy state that logs what the policy would have done without enforcing it — intended as a staging step before enforcement, not a permanent state.
What If tool
An Entra admin center simulator that shows which Conditional Access policies apply to a hypothetical sign-in and what the outcome would be.
Legacy authentication
Older protocols (POP, IMAP, SMTP AUTH) that cannot perform modern authentication and therefore bypass MFA entirely unless blocked.
Policy drift
The gradual divergence of a deployed policy set from the template it was created from, caused by untracked one-off changes.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the minimum Conditional Access policies every tenant needs?

Six: MFA for all users, blocking legacy authentication, stricter MFA for admin roles, device compliance for sensitive apps, risk- or location-based restriction, and session controls for unmanaged devices. Anything beyond that should map to a specific named risk.

How do I clean up and consolidate Conditional Access policies?

Export the current set, group policies by the condition they actually test rather than their name, and merge duplicates that differ only by target app or group into one policy with broader scope. Enforce or delete every report-only policy older than a few weeks.

How do I analyse Conditional Access report-only logs?

Use the Conditional Access Insights and reporting workbook filtered to the specific policy to see what it would have blocked. Review on a schedule — a report-only policy without a review date tends to stay report-only indefinitely.

How do MSPs keep Conditional Access consistent across customer tenants?

A policy document copied once is not enough, because tenants drift independently after deployment. Centralised management tooling that reapplies a template and flags divergence closes that gap; a static reference document cannot detect drift.
