IT Security Budget Buy-In: The Price of "We'll Revisit Next Quarter"

November 2026 • By Philipp Schmidt
SecurityIT BudgetCase StudySMBBest Practices

Management buy-in for an IT security budget usually stalls on one argument: nothing bad has happened yet. Years without an incident get treated as proof that the current setup works, while the cost of delayed work stays invisible.

This is a deliberately generalised case analysis. Customer-identifying details, direct quotations, and case figures have been omitted.

The Environment Nobody Wanted to Touch

The starting point will be familiar to many IT teams: unsupported servers, separately configured PCs, users with more rights than they need, removable media treated as a backup plan, and a few settings that depend on one person remembering them.

None of this is exotic. It is what accumulates when a small business keeps working around ageing systems because there has not yet been a visible failure.

What I Recommended, and What It Was Worth

The recommendations were straightforward: move to supported platforms, protect sign-ins, manage devices, and keep a backup that is separate from the systems it protects.

The discussion was familiar too. Email still worked. The old servers still started. The backup job still reported success. Each point looked easier to defer than to fund.

The decision became: revisit next quarter.

Why Is "Nothing Bad Has Happened Yet" the Most Expensive Sentence in IT?

Over time, minor failures became more frequent: a service had to be rebuilt, a device was replaced after malware, an old component was kept alive for one more season, and nobody could say with confidence what the backup actually contained.

Preventive work is hard to value precisely because its success is quiet. When it works, there is no outage to point at. That does not make the work optional.

What Documentation Actually Buys You

Keep recommendations in writing: the risk, the proposed control, the expected cost, and the decision. In a difficult conversation, a decision trail is more useful than anyone's memory of it.

Documentation does not turn a refused control into a working one. It does make the next decision clearer. Part 2 looks at the recovery controls that matter once prevention has already failed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do small businesses decline security recommendations they can afford?

The price is rarely the real objection. What's actually happening is survivorship bias: years without an incident get read as evidence the setup works, when it's often evidence someone's been preventing incidents without anyone noticing.

How should a small business assess the cost of Microsoft 365 security?

Use current pricing from your reseller or Microsoft agreement, then compare it with the business impact of the specific risks you are leaving open. A generic per-user number is not a substitute for that calculation.

How do I get management buy-in for an IT security budget?

Tie every recommendation to a specific business consequence, not a technical feature: a person, a date, a number. Put it in writing. A good pitch doesn't guarantee buy-in, but the paper trail protects you either way.

Is an unsupported end-of-life server actually risky if it's “been fine for years”?

Years without failure change nothing about the underlying risk: no security patches, no vendor support, and an increasingly slow path back if it does fail. “Fine for years” describes the past, not the odds going forward.
